## What happened

On December 31, 2025, the White House signed a proclamation postponing the scheduled increase in Section 232 tariffs on imported upholstered furniture, kitchen cabinets and vanities by one year. The increase had been due on January 1, 2026. It is now [scheduled for January 1, 2027](https://mohawkglobal.com/word-from-the-white-house/furniture-and-cabinet-imports-section-232-tariff-increase-delayed-to-2027/).

The current 25% duty did not go away. It [stays at 25% through December 31, 2026](https://gwlcorp.com/1941-2/). What was deferred is the step up: upholstered wooden furniture was set to move from 25% to 30%, and kitchen cabinets and vanities from 25% to 50%, according to the customs brokers who published the HTS breakdown in early January.

We are writing about a January proclamation in September on purpose. The delay bought a year, and most of that year is gone. From a furniture retailer's chair, the interesting date was never January 1. It is the day the last container that clears before January 1 has to be ordered.

## Why it matters on the floor

Upholstery is a special-order business. A customer picks a frame, a cover and a leg finish, and the piece is built after the sale. Depending on the vendor, that is four to sixteen weeks from order to dock, plus whatever the freight lane adds. A retailer selling an imported sofa in late October is, in practice, deciding which side of the tariff line it lands on.

That turns a policy date into three operational questions that most stores answer by feel:

- **Which vendors are actually exposed?** Not "imported or domestic" on the brochure, but which price lists carry Section 232 product, and which of those vendors have already announced a January price move.
- **What is on order right now, and when is it due?** If a purchase order for a 30-piece container ships late, the tariff on it changes. Someone should know which POs those are before the vendor's confirmation email arrives, not after.
- **How does the quote hold up?** A price quoted in November for a special order that ships in February is a promise made on this year's cost. Either the quote has a validity date and a clause, or the margin absorbs the difference.

None of this is new. Tariff changes in 2018 and 2025 asked the same questions. What has changed is how many stores now have the data to answer them without a weekend and a spreadsheet.

## What technology changes about it

Honestly, less than the vendors selling "AI for tariffs" would like. No software knows what the administration will do in December. What software can do is make the exposure visible early and keep it visible.

The useful version looks unglamorous. A system that holds every vendor price list as a dated version, so you can see exactly which items and options carry which cost and when the next list takes effect. A purchase-order record that carries the manufacturer's confirmed ship date, not the date you hoped for, and flags the ones sliding toward the line. A quote that knows its own expiry. A report that answers "how much of my open special-order book is on tariffed product" without exporting three things into one workbook.

Where AI genuinely earns a place is in the intake, not the forecast. Vendor confirmations arrive as PDFs and emails in a dozen formats. Reading those automatically, matching them to the right PO and updating the promise date is exactly the kind of repetitive reading that models do well and people do badly at 5 p.m. on a Friday. The judgement, whether to pull an order forward or reprice a floor model, stays with a buyer. It should.

The trap is the opposite of the pitch. Retailers that respond to tariff uncertainty by buying a dashboard and leaving the underlying paperwork on paper end up with a very confident chart built on last month's numbers.

## One thing to do this week

Pull your open purchase orders for imported upholstery, cabinets and vanities, and sort them by confirmed ship date. Anything without a confirmed date gets an email to the vendor today. Anything confirmed for late December gets a phone call. That list, kept current, is the whole tariff strategy for a store our size. Everything else is commentary.
